Apparel Demand & Inventory Model

A monthly apparel demand forecast for the United States and Canada, driven by temperature. Fourteen years of Berkeley Earth land-temperature data feed a demand model in Excel, FORECAST.ETS projects each country's temperature forward, and the demand that follows sets the inventory a buyer would need to commit to.

Download the Workbook US Forecast CSV Canada Forecast CSV

What the Model Covers

The model runs on two markets, the United States and Canada, which are the two the Berkeley Earth extract could be joined to reliably. Each gets fourteen years of monthly average land temperature, a demand curve built on top of it, and a forecast carried fourteen years forward from there.

From Temperature to Demand

Demand has two arms. Winter apparel sells harder the further temperature falls below 100°F, summer apparel the further it climbs above 50°F, and each is scaled by a market-size factor for the country and a seasonal factor for the month. Winter demand is gated off between March and August, so the two arms do not both run in the shoulder months.

Forecasting with FORECAST.ETS

Excel's FORECAST.ETS fits exponential triple smoothing across three components: level, trend and a repeating seasonal cycle. With the seasonality argument set to 12, it reads the annual temperature cycle out of the history and carries it forward, so a forecast January looks like a January rather than an extension of a straight line. Each country's 164 months of history produce 164 months of forecast.

There are two defensible ways to use it here, and the workbook contains both. Canada's forecast runs FORECAST.ETS directly on the demand series. The United States forecasts temperature and applies the demand formulas to the result. This page charts the second version for both countries, so the two are computed the same way and can be compared. Against Canada's stored numbers that reconstruction correlates at r = 0.99, with a mean absolute difference of 39 units per month, about 18% of Canada's monthly average. It runs somewhat higher overall, because smoothing the demand series directly pulls its peaks down.

Canada's stored forecast is preserved as-is in canada_forecast.csv.

The Inventory Decision

Stock is committed a month ahead at forecast demand plus a buffer. The realised buffer is drawn from a normal distribution centred on the target with an 8% standard deviation, so the order rarely matches demand exactly. Sales are the lesser of demand and inventory, anything short of demand is a lost sale, and anything above it is excess inventory. Total cost is lost sales times the stockout cost plus excess units times the holding cost.

The solver walks the buffer from 0% to 20% in steps of two points. At each step it runs the stocking decision 60 times across both forecast horizons and averages the total cost, then reports the cheapest step. Changing either cost below re-runs the whole sweep, since the answer depends on how the two costs compare rather than on their absolute size.

Raise the stockout cost and the optimal buffer climbs; raise the holding cost and it falls. At $15 and $3 the curve is nearly flat between 6% and 10%, which is the more useful finding. The workbook's 10% default is already close to optimal, and precision there buys very little.

How Much That Answer Moves

The stocking outcome is a draw, not a fixed result. Running the decision 500 times over both forecast horizons at the 10% buffer gives the range a buyer would actually live inside.

Known Limitations

  • The Berkeley Earth history ends in September 2013, so the forecast projects from a record that is now a decade old rather than from current conditions.
  • A country is represented by a single national monthly average. Canada spans several climates and one number for the whole country flattens all of them.
  • Demand responds to temperature, seasonality and market size, and to nothing else. There is no price, no promotion, no competitor behaviour, no shift in consumer preference, and no inventory carried from one month into the next.
  • The country and season factors are assumed rather than estimated. Canada's 1.2 and the monthly multipliers were chosen to be plausible, and no sales data was fitted to them, so demand levels are meaningful relative to each other rather than as units anyone would order against.
  • Demand is modelled as two linear kinks. Combined with the March to August winter gate and the 50°F summer threshold, that leaves a dead zone: US March demand comes out at zero, because winter is switched off and the month averages just under 50°F. That is a property of the curve, not a claim about March.
